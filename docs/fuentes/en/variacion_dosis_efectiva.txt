Heart rate.
The only data collected systematically were the heart rates of the
commanders. Figure 1 shows the heart rates of each of the five astronauts
on the Apollo missions during the descent to the lunar surface. The mean heart rate 

=== PAGE ===
across these missions was approximately 96 beats per minute (bpm; range: 66–
144).
Heart rates remained stable until the time of peak G load, when
some commanders apparently experienced a moderate increase of 10 to 20 bpm;
however, heart rate did not decrease substantially when G loads were reduced
(Figure 2). The mean heart rate increased, reaching a peak of nearly 120 bpm near the
time of lunar landing (range: 94–153).
During the lunar ascent phase, the mean heart rate of the five Apollo mission astronauts
was approximately 88 beats per minute (range: 70–109) (Figure 3). Although
there was an initial increase in heart rate at liftoff, the average
response did not increase over time. The mean heart rate recorded during
the ascent phase was similar to that observed during the initial stage of the descent phase (0.3 to
0.4 G), but turned out to be about 20 beats per minute lower than that recorded during the final part of
the descent, when G forces were lower. That is, unlike what occurred during
descent, the heart rate response during ascent did not increase over time.
Although the initial increase in heart rate (10–20 beats per minute) probably
included orthostatic and psychological components, the fact that heart rate
remained stable afterward suggests that, despite the increase in G forces, the effects
of the stress associated with ascent did not intensify.
Nearly all crew members reported symptoms resulting from the headward fluid shift
—such as a sensation of head congestion— during
adaptation to spaceflight; to treat this, nasal drops were used to
relieve the congestion until the symptoms resolved. However, most astronauts
did not experience nausea, vomiting, or disorientation during their mission.

Temperature.
Ranges and behavior in space
• Usual resting temperature (based on long-duration missions). After about 2.5 months
in orbit, core temperature gradually rises from the terrestrial 37.0 °C until it stabilizes
near 38.0 °C. This level persists until they return to Earth.
• Temperature during exercise. When training in microgravity, body heat rises much
faster than on Earth. It is common for it to exceed 40.0 °C during physical routines, which on
Earth would be equivalent to a very high fever.

=== PAGE ===
• Medical alert criterion. Because their baseline rises, aerospace physicians do not assess
fever using the fixed terrestrial threshold of 38.0 °C. Instead, they watch for abrupt increases above
their new space mean or the presence of other infectious symptoms.
Why "space fever" occurs
• Lack of convection and slow evaporation. On Earth, hot air rises and sweat evaporates
quickly to cool us. In space there is no gravity to move the air, and sweat accumulates
on the skin instead of evaporating easily.
• Mild inflammatory response. Studies such as the European Space Agency (ESA) Thermolab project
and research published in Scientific Reports found that the body in space produces
more inflammation-related proteins (such as interleukin-1), keeping the body
in an elevated metabolic state.

Mood.
Main scales used
1. POMS (Profile of Mood States)
- Assesses six dimensions of mood.
- Tension, depression, anger, vigor, fatigue, and confusion.
- Allows identification of emotional changes during space missions.
2. PANAS (Positive and Negative Affect Schedule)
- Measures positive and negative emotions.
- For example: enthusiasm, joy, nervousness, fear, and irritability.
- Helps in understanding the emotional state of astronauts.
3. AMS (Alertness and Mood Survey)
- Assesses alertness and mood.
- Includes aspects such as boredom, stress, sadness, tiredness, and sleep quality.
- It was designed to study the conditions of astronauts during spaceflight.
4. VAS (Visual Analog Scales)

4. VAS (Visual Analog Scales)
- These are visual scales on which astronauts rate how they feel.
- They can measure stress, tiredness, energy, and mood.
- They allow quick assessments
What are they used for in space?
- Detecting stress and emotional changes.
- Identifying symptoms of isolation and sadness.
- Assessing psychological fatigue.
- Preventing mental health problems.
- Improving coexistence and teamwork.
