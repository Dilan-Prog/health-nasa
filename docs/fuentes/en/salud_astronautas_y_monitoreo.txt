 Main hazards of spaceflight 
NASA's Human Research Program groups the main hazards of spaceflight into five categories. They do not act 
in isolation: they can combine and increase their effects on health and performance. 
Hazard Description 
Space Radiation 
High-energy particles and radiation that can damage cells, DNA, and electronic components. 
Isolation and Confinement 
Prolonged stays in confined spaces can affect sleep, mental health, and interpersonal relations. 
Distance from Earth Hinders communication, resupply, and immediate medical care. 
Gravity Fields 
Microgravity changes the function of bones, muscles, the cardiovascular system, balance, and orientation. 
Hostile/Closed Environments 
Pressure, temperature, oxygen, carbon dioxide, noise, and microorganisms must be controlled. 
1. Risks to the body systems 
1.1 Cardiovascular and circulatory system 
In microgravity, body fluids shift toward the chest and head, because the gravitational gradient to which the body is accustomed 
no longer acts in the same way. 
• Redistribution of blood and other body fluids; facial swelling. 
• Changes in plasma volume and in blood pressure regulation. 
• Changes in the function of the heart and blood vessels. 
• Possible orthostatic intolerance on return to Earth's gravity.• The risk of venous flow disorders and 
thrombosis is under investigation. 
 
1.2 Musculoskeletal system: bones and muscles 
Bones and muscles receive less mechanical loading in microgravity. As the work they do to support the body decreases, 
they can lose mass, strength, and density. 
• Decrease in bone mineral density and loss of minerals such as calcium and phosphorus. 
• Greater bone fragility and risk of fractures. 
• Loss of muscle mass and strength, especially in postural muscles. 
• Expansion of the intervertebral discs and temporary lengthening of the spine, which can be accompanied by back pain. 
• Increased urinary excretion of calcium, which can contribute to the risk of kidney stones.

1.3 Nervous and sensory system 
The vestibular system of the inner ear contributes to balance and orientation. In microgravity, the brain receives signals different 
from the usual ones and needs to adapt. 
• Space motion sickness, nausea, and disorientation. 
• Impaired hand-eye coordination and gait. 
• Changes in the perception of motion and spatial orientation. 
• Temporary difficulty moving around or controlling vehicles and tools. 
 
1.4 Visual and ocular system: SANS 
Spaceflight Associated Neuro-ocular Syndrome (SANS) comprises changes in structures of the eye and the visual system that 
have been observed in some astronauts, especially on long-duration missions. 
• Changes in the shape of the eyeball and in the back of the eye. 
• Alterations of the optic nerve and of retinal structures. 
• Changes in focusing ability and in visual refraction. 
• Blurred vision or difficulty focusing on nearby objects. 
 
1.5 Immune system 
Microgravity, radiation, physical and psychological stress, and sleep changes can modify the immune response. 
• Changes in the activity of white blood cells, T lymphocytes, and NK cells. 
• Alterations in the regulation of inflammatory substances. 
• Possible greater susceptibility to certain infections. 
• Reactivation of latent viruses, including some viruses of the herpes group. 
 
1.6 Respiratory system and oxygenation 
The spacecraft and spacesuits must maintain a breathable mixture and control pressure, oxygen, and carbon dioxide. 
• Hypoxia: decrease in the oxygen available to the tissues. 
• Hypercapnia: excessive increase of carbon dioxide in the body. 
• Changes in ventilation and gas exchange. 
• Fatigue, headache, and decreased concentration when oxygenation or ventilation is inadequate. 
 
1.7 Blood and hematologic system 
Spaceflight can modify plasma volume and some blood cell indicators. 
• Changes in plasma volume and in red blood cell mass. 
• Variations in hemoglobin and in oxygen transport. 
• Changes in indicators of inflammation and immune response.

1.8 Mental health and behavior 
Isolation, confinement, distance from family, workload, and uncertainty can produce psychological stress. 
• Anxiety, irritability, and prolonged stress. 
• Difficulties with concentration, memory, and decision-making. 
• Mental fatigue and interpersonal conflicts. 
• Risk of errors when attention is impaired. 
. 
1.9 Sleep and circadian rhythm 
The circadian rhythm is the biological clock that regulates sleep-wake cycles. Changes in lighting and schedules can make it difficult to 
synchronize. 
• Alterations in sleep duration and quality. 
• Changes in alertness and daytime sleepiness. 
• Fatigue, reduced concentration, and possible operational errors. 
 
2. Space radiation: a priority risk 
Space radiation consists of high-energy particles. Some can pass through shielding materials and deposit 
energy in tissues. Its effects depend on the dose, the type of particle, the duration of exposure, solar activity, and 
individual characteristics. 
Source Description 
Galactic Cosmic Rays 
(GCR) 
High-energy particles originating from outside the solar system; some are associated with phenomena such as 
supernovae. 
Solar Particle Events 
(SPE) Episodes of solar activity that can increase exposure to energetic particles. 
Coronal Mass Ejections 
(CME) Expulsions of plasma and magnetic fields from the Sun that can modify the space environment. 
Possible health effects 
• DNA damage and cellular alterations. 
• Increased long-term cancer risk. 
• Increased risk of cataracts. 
• Possible cardiovascular and neurological effects that remain under investigation.

3. Effects of the space environment on electronic devices 
Equipment that works correctly on Earth will not necessarily work the same way in space. Electronic components 
must be designed and tested considering radiation, temperature, microgravity, interference, pressure, power, and communications. 
3.1 Space radiation 
Energetic particles can interact with semiconductors and circuits, causing transient errors or cumulative degradation. 
• SEU (Single Event Upset): a particle changes a logic state in memory and can alter a data value. 
• SET (Single Event Transient): a momentary electrical disturbance appears that can produce an erroneous reading. 
• SEL (Single Event Latch-up): an unwanted electrical path is activated, with a possible increase in current and damage. 
• TID (Total Ionizing Dose): the accumulated ionizing dose progressively degrades certain components. 
Implication and prevention: Monitoring may involve corrupted data, resets, loss of measurements, or false alarms. Suitable 
components, error correction, redundancy, and radiation testing are required. 
3.2 Extreme temperatures 
The temperature of the equipment depends on its location, orientation, solar illumination, and the spacecraft's thermal control. 
• Overheating of circuits. 
• Changes in sensor accuracy. 
• Variation in battery performance. 
• Deterioration of connections and reduced service life. 
Implication and prevention: The design must include selection of suitable components, thermal control, and temperature compensation 
in the sensors. 
3.3 Microgravity 
Microgravity does not by itself keep an electronic circuit from working, but it changes the physical handling of the equipment and of 
fluids. 
• Loose devices or cables can drift. 
• Connectors can come loose if they are not secured. 
• Fluids do not behave as they do on Earth. 
• Heat dissipation and ventilation must be designed without relying on natural terrestrial convection. 
Implication and prevention: The equipment must be secured, use secure connectors, and maintain reliable contact between sensors and skin. 
3.4 Electromagnetic interference 
Electronic equipment can generate or receive interference that manifests as electrical noise. 
• Noise in ECG and other signals. 
• Loss or distortion of data. 
• Interference among sensors, processors, and communications. 
Implication and prevention: Electromagnetic compatibility, filtering, circuit design, and appropriate wiring are required.

3.5 Pressure and environmental conditions 
Equipment inside the spacecraft operates in a controlled atmosphere; equipment used outside needs protection against vacuum and 
extreme conditions. 
• Stress on seals, materials, and encapsulation. 
• Heat dissipation problems. 
• Risk of condensation under specific conditions. 
• Deterioration of components exposed to the environment. 
Implication and prevention: The design must clearly specify whether the device will operate inside the spacecraft, inside the suit, or 
outside. 
3.6 Batteries and electrical power 
Continuous operation depends on a reliable power source and efficient management. 
• Changes in capacity due to temperature and aging. 
• Power interruption. 
• Data loss during shutdowns. 
• Errors when estimating the charge level. 
Implication and prevention: Power management, low-battery alerts, and storage that preserves data during 
interruptions are recommended. 
3.7 Communication and data transmission 
On distant missions, distance can cause significant delays in communication with Earth. 
• Transmission delay. 
• Packet loss. 
• Temporary interruptions. 
• Delay in receiving instructions from the ground medical team. 
 
4. Treatments 
 
 
Before each mission, astronauts undergo a series of medical tests to establish a pre-flight baseline, 
using key health parameters such as aerobic capacity, agility, strength, and endurance. These pre-flight measurements 
and routine health monitoring aboard the space station help NASA's astronaut strength, conditioning, and rehabilitation specialists 
(ASCR) personalize and adjust the astronauts' exercise plans to optimize their performance. 
 
Recovery begins on the first day back on Earth with medical evaluations and a progression through exercises that improve 
mobility, balance, flexibility, aerobic conditioning, strength, endurance, and the body's ability to sense its 
position and movement. Returning astronauts undergo the same pre-flight tests to assess their health status. 
These results serve as the basis for a personalized reconditioning program that seeks to restore their physical performance 
safely and progressively until they reach their pre-flight level. The ASCR adapt each rehabilitation plan according to the astronaut's condition, 
test results, and medical status. As crew members progress through the program

post-flight, each aspect of their functional recovery is carefully monitored and addressed to ensure a smooth transition 
to daily life and prepare them for future missions. 
 
Post-flight reconditioning is unique to each astronaut and varies from person to person. Although most crew members 
reach their pre-flight fitness level within the first 45 days after returning to Earth, it is not uncommon 
for NASA to extend reconditioning programs to meet each astronaut's specific needs and recovery 
pace. 
What medical follow-up do astronauts receive on return? 
After landing, astronauts go through a recovery program that generally includes: 
Initial medical evaluation: general review of their health status. 
Physical tests: assessment of strength, balance, coordination, and endurance. 
Laboratory studies: analyses to detect possible abnormalities. 
Physical rehabilitation: exercises to recover muscular and cardiovascular capacities. 
Psychological follow-up: support for readjusting to life on Earth. 
Long-term surveillance: checkups to detect possible consequences of radiation exposure and time spent in space.
